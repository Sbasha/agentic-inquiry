# Format notes for stories

Classify form before applying format conventions.
A memo, report, project page, article, or talk may contain a story without becoming story-primary.
When the artifact is argument-primary, use its argument or documentation contract and keep the story bounded.

## Article or essay

Let the story establish its own pace.
Headings are optional and should mark real movements, not package lessons.
Do not add a summary ending.

## Technical incident or project story

Preserve timeline, system state, decisions, evidence, and uncertainty.
Separate what participants knew at the time from what became clear later.
Do not turn the incident into a triumph narrative or make the product the hero.

## Talk

Write for speech and memory without manufacturing vulnerability or quotable slogans.
Confirm which details may be spoken publicly and which visuals or names require consent.
An argument-primary talk may state its answer early while an embedded story unfolds normally.

## Slide deck

Use slides to anchor moments, evidence, or transitions rather than transcribing the narration.
Do not force every slide title into a claim when the deck is story-primary.
Read-alone decks need enough context to preserve sequence without the speaker.

## Memo or report

Most are argument-primary and should use their expository contract.
If a story is included, label its evidentiary role and keep the recommendation separate from narrative effect.
Do not hide the decision behind chronology.

## Project page

Use a short story only when origin, failure, or change helps the reader understand the artifact.
Keep current status, evidence, limitations, and access paths easy to find.
Do not let the story replace inspectable project facts.

## Personal or cultural piece

Preserve the exact subject, moment, reaction, and source of feeling.
Do not force a professional analogy, universal claim, or lesson.
Humor, fragments, and exclamation are welcome when they came from the source rather than the editing process.
