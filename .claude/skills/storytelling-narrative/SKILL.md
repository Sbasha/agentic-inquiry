# Storytelling and narrative

Help the user find and shape the story already present in their material.
Do not invent narrative substrate or force an argument into a story arc.

## Confirm the form

A story primarily answers: what happened, what was it like, what changed, or why does this moment matter?
An argument primarily asks the reader to believe or do something because reasons and evidence support it.

Subject does not decide form.
A technical incident can be a story.
A personal or cultural piece can be an argument.

If an event sequence could be replaced by bullets without meaningful loss, it may be evidence inside an argument rather than a story.
If both forms are indispensable, identify the primary form before structuring.
Route argument-primary work to the applicable argument workflow.

Minto does not apply to stories.
Do not use Pyramid Principle, SCQA, answer-first grouping, or a forced thesis to organize narrative movement.

## Accept short direction

The user may provide only:

- direction;
- specific events, scenes, details, or observations;
- a desired theme or narrative center;
- optional audience, format, tone, disclosure, or exclusion notes.

Play back one sentence naming the story you believe they want to tell.
Ask one to three focused questions only when missing material would require you to invent a scene, sequence, feeling, causal link, meaning, or disclosure decision.
Do not run a long interview by default.

## Build from confirmed material

Separate:

- confirmed events and chronology;
- remembered dialogue and its confidence;
- direct observation;
- later interpretation;
- unresolved gaps;
- details that must not be inferred;
- people whose consent or privacy matters.

Never invent a scene, quotation, sensory detail, emotion, causation, conflict, or transformation.
Do not turn hardship, identity, vulnerability, or another person's experience into a hook.

## Find the narrative center

Identify the smallest movement that makes the material worth telling.
Useful elements may include a starting condition, event, turn, consequence, aftermath, image, or reflection.
None is mandatory.

A story may be quiet, unresolved, observational, episodic, funny, or primarily about attention.
It does not need danger, an antagonist, a call to action, a professional lesson, a five-second transformation, or a final moral.
Do not inflate the stakes to make the piece feel important.

If several narrative centers are plausible, present at most three short options and ask the user to choose.
If one clearly follows from the supplied material, proceed with it provisionally and let the user react.

## Choose only a fitting lens

Read `references/narrative-frameworks.md` when the material benefits from a named story lens.
Treat every lens as optional.
The material may need simple chronological order, controlled nonlinearity, a scene and reflection, or no named framework at all.

Read `references/hooks-and-moments.md` only when the opening or a key moment needs attention.
Read `references/formats.md` when the delivery format changes narrative behavior.
Read `references/critique.md` for an existing draft or final story review.

## Draft privately

- Use concrete details the user supplied.
- Preserve uncertainty in memory and chronology.
- Keep the point of view stable unless the user approves a change.
- Let paragraphs and fragments follow the movement of the story.
- Preserve humor, excitement, irritation, silence, and understatement when they are in the material.
- Attach energy to the supplied object, action, or exact reaction.
  Do not add a free-standing reaction tag such as `What a move`, `What a pleasure`, `So good`, or `I loved it` unless the user supplied that phrase.
  When the user asks for a little life but supplies no exact reaction phrase, keep the evaluation grammatically attached to the concrete detail.
- Do not manufacture lyrical language, staged drama, suspense, intimacy, or sentiment.
- Do not explain the meaning after the story has already conveyed it.
- Stop where the story naturally lands, including an unresolved image or question when appropriate.

The first draft remains private.
If the story is intended for public use and is identity-bearing or opinion-bearing, keep it private until the represented human explicitly approves it.
Use `rewrite-slop` only as a final surface pass that preserves sequence, meaning, and voice.

## Review

Verify:

- every scene and detail comes from supplied material;
- the narrative center belongs to the user or is clearly marked as a proposal;
- the sequence does not create a false causal claim;
- no person is reduced to a device for the narrator's growth;
- no personal material was made more revealing for impact;
- the story was not forced into Minto or an argument conclusion;
- fragments and pacing feel earned rather than staged;
- the ending completes the movement without packaging a lesson.
